If you've landed on this article, it may be because relationships also create stress, fear, or anxiety for you — despite your **desire to connect**.

Many people **feel shame and confusion in the face of these reactions, as if something were wrong with them**. This article aims to help you connect what you experience in relationships today with an understanding of the post-traumatic stress that may be **at its root**.

As I already described in [another article](https://www.benedictedonet-psyenligne.com/en/comment-te-relies-tu-aux-autres/), we can have **a secure or insecure attachment** depending on how we experienced **our earliest attachment bonds**. To understand why, we need to understand our need for safety.

## The need for safety

A fetus, a baby, a child needs safety: regularity, **reliability in the bond**, adults who are available and able to welcome their emotions. This is what Winnicott called “holding” — being **held**, contained, when we experience strong emotions.

This emotional safety is what **then allows us to explore the world**, knowing we have a secure base to return to if needed.

![](/images/cms-library/caleb-woods-vzildyoqn_u-unsplash.jpg)

## The legacy of insecurity

Perhaps as you grew up, the adults around you did not themselves have the capacity to name and welcome their own emotions — and even less those of their children. The point is not to judge or **blame past generations**, but to recognize what they themselves never received and were **therefore unable to pass on to the next generation**.

Awareness of the real care a child needs is recent. Only a few decades ago, people in distress were still treated with cold showers in psychiatric hospitals. Emotions were swept under the rug.

In previous generations, many practices that create insecurity were passed down and **normalized** in France (and in many other countries).

Here are some of these practices, which have left traces of post-traumatic stress in the following generation:

### Physical violence

These are the most obvious, and also the most trivialized — **“a slap never hurt anyone,”** we still often hear.

And yet it does. It was only in **2019** that a law was passed in France banning ordinary educational violence.

Hitting a child **has physical and emotional consequences**:

fear of the other develops, mechanisms of **immobility take hold**, and above all the belief takes root that the person meant to care for us **is not a reliable source of emotional safety.**

![](/images/cms-library/chinh-le-duc-tv1qyuttxj8-unsplash.jpg)

## Why?

Because when a child is punished with physical (or verbal) violence, **the body remembers**. It remembers, and in the future that bodily memory can be reactivated simply by being in contact with another human being — especially in intimacy.

**Studies on corporal punishment show an association with:**

more aggression in the child,

more anxiety and depressive disorders,

more behavioural problems,

a damaged parent–child relationship,

and an increased risk of antisocial behaviour.

Psychologically, a slap (for example) **breaks affective security**: the adult meant to protect becomes **a source of fear**.

Repeated, it can create what is called **“disorganized”** attachment, where the child is **caught between the need for closeness and fear of the attachment figure**.

If you have lived through something similar, this is precisely what can replay in your current relationships, **without you understanding why**:

you want to attach, to move toward the other — and at the same time you sense an inexplicable danger, a fear of connection.

Memory has done its job. It remembers. And your body protects itself.

### Verbal violence

Shouting, humiliating, insulting, threatening, comparing a child unfavourably to another:

these forms of violence leave no visible mark, but they hit the same thing as physical violence — **the sense of safety.**

The child grows up with **hypervigilance to tone of voice**, to signs of anger, to the silences that precede an explosion.

They learn to **anticipate the other’s mood** rather than express their own.

As an adult, this mechanism replays in couple life: a slightly sharp tone, a silence, a disagreement **can reactivate an inner alarm out of proportion** to the present situation — because the body reacts to what it learned to dread, not only to what is actually happening today.

I also want to name that this violence may have been transmitted by:

Other children in nursery school, primary school, middle school, high school, summer camp — any group situation where a child may experience a form of bullying.

Also, some people have experienced educational violence from teachers during their schooling, or from a boss at work, leaving lasting traces.

### Absence

Violence is not required to create insecurity: absence is enough. Physical absence — a parent who is rarely present, withdrawn — or **emotional absence** — a parent who is physically present but **unavailable** to welcome what I feel.

The child then learns that their needs **will not always find a response**, or that it is better **not to have too many.** They may develop **early** autonomy that looks solid on the surface, but is built on the idea that **you cannot really count on the other**.

In adulthood, this can show up as difficulty asking for help, **discomfort with intimacy**, or on the contrary an **intense fear** of being left aside again as soon as a partner pulls away even a little.

### When a parent projects their emotional needs onto their child

Here, the roles reverse: the child becomes the person the parent leans on emotionally — to be reassured, comforted, valued. This is sometimes called **parentification**.

The child learns very early to be attentive to the adult’s emotions, hypervigilant, sometimes to manage them, at the expense of **their own needs**, which take second place or even become invisible to themselves.

As an adult, this person is often the one who **takes care of everyone**, who struggles to receive, and who **feels guilty as soon as they prioritize their own needs in a relationship**.

![](/images/cms-library/louis-galvez-i8gqvrdcxzy-unsplash.jpg)

### Not adapting to the child

Treating a child like a thing rather than a person: **not letting them express themselves**, speak at the table, ask questions — on the pretext that they are “**too young**” for their opinion to matter.

This message, repeated, teaches the child that their voice and needs **do not really have value, and that they must adapt to the other rather than the other way around**.

In a couple, this can lead to great difficulty setting boundaries, expressing disagreement, or even knowing what one wants — because one never really learned that it mattered.

## To conclude

If you recognize yourself in one or more of these situations, **it is not because you are “too sensitive” or “complicated”** in love. These are entirely logical responses from a nervous system that learned, very early, that **connection could be dangerous**, unpredictable, or conditioned on meeting someone else’s needs. Post-traumatic stress from this kind of experience can persist and block secure, trusting romantic relationships.

The good news is that **what was learned can be relearned**. Attachment is not fixed: with time, **awareness of these mechanisms**, and sometimes therapeutic support, it is possible to gradually build a sense of safety — within yourself, and in your bond with another.
